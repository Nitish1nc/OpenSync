# Core-Regulation as a High-Leverage Sensorimotor System: A Deep Audit of Your Walking, “Micro-Squat”, Burst-Speed, Diaphragm and Voice Observations

## What I searched for

I treated your description as a **multi-system motor-control problem**, not as “core training”. The search covered gait initiation, anticipatory postural adjustments, trunk–pelvis dissociation, abdominal bracing, pelvic-floor/diaphragm coordination, walking-speed effects, vestibular/proprioceptive reweighting, reaction time, acute arousal, post-activation effects, vocal projection, subglottal pressure and posture.

The central hypothesis I tested was this:

> **Can deliberately maintaining a regulated trunk while forcing the pelvis/legs/whole body through changing acceleration, vertical displacement and speed create a disproportionately large sensorimotor, respiratory and arousal effect?**

The answer is: **there is surprisingly strong support for several pieces of that model, but the exact combination you are doing has barely been studied as a unified intervention.**

The most useful way to think about it is not “core strength”. It is **rapid control of a moving reference frame**.

## The strongest finding: the trunk is not merely stabilising movement — it can prepare the movement

Your observation that you can be walking casually, suddenly “load” the core, and then feel as though the body is immediately ready to launch has a very close analogue in gait-initiation research.

When people initiate walking, trunk muscles can change activity **before the centre of pressure even begins its backward shift**. In one experiment, rectus abdominis and oblique activation began roughly 27–82 ms before the mechanically obvious initiation of gait, while erector-spinae activity changed even earlier in some participants. The authors interpreted these trunk adjustments as part of linking the body segments to the pelvis as propulsion begins. citeturn890919search1turn394040search1

That matters because your phrase “the core directly launches” is **subjectively reasonable but mechanically needs one correction**: your torso cannot accelerate forward without external impulse from the ground. What can happen, however, is that the trunk is organised *before* the visible step, so that when leg-ground forces appear, the rest of the body is already configured to transmit them efficiently. citeturn890919search1turn890919search2

In rapid gait initiation, people deliberately create a centre-of-pressure/centre-of-mass separation to accelerate the body. Faster initiation changes how that instability is generated; one study found different whole-body strategies when subjects were instructed to initiate gait rapidly. citeturn890919search2

That gives us a much better interpretation of what you are sensing:

**You may not be “building momentum” first. You may be reducing the organisational delay between intention and useful ground-reaction force.**

That is a completely different phenomenon.

And preparation matters. In rapid-stepping experiments, poor preparation delayed trunk/hip muscle activation and lengthened stepping time. citeturn394040search3

So your sensation of becoming “ready” before the step is not mystical. **Pre-movement organisation is literally measurable.**

## Your up–down “micro-squat walking” may be making gait much more sensorimotor-dense

This part of your description is unusually interesting:

You keep walking, but deliberately allow the torso to move vertically by repeatedly introducing very small knee/hip flexion, while simultaneously demanding that the core remain organised.

That changes several things at once.

Ordinary walking already changes vertical centre-of-mass excursion with speed. In one experiment, vertical COM displacement increased from about **2.74 cm at the slowest speed to 4.83 cm at 1.6 m/s**, while mediolateral displacement decreased as walking became faster. citeturn890919search6

Trunk-muscle activation also rises with walking speed. In healthy men walking from 2 to 6 km/h, mean trunk EMG generally increased and became increasingly phasic as speed rose. citeturn890919search9

Deep trunk muscles do not simply turn on uniformly. Fine-wire EMG shows that transversus abdominis, superficial abdominals and spinal muscles have **different locomotor and respiratory activation patterns**, and these patterns change as walking becomes running and speed increases. citeturn394040search6

Now add deliberate crouching.

In 19 adult men, progressively crouched walking increased activation across almost all measured lower-limb muscles, with especially strong increases in thigh and gluteal muscles. citeturn890919search7

So your tiny vertical pulses are likely increasing the amount of muscular work and joint torque required compared with ordinary walking. But the more interesting piece is that you're **not simply crouching**: you're continuously asking the trunk to preserve orientation while the pelvis changes height.

That creates what motor-control researchers would call a **coordination problem**.

A 2024 experiment showed that only 20 minutes of practice could improve people's ability to move the pelvis independently of the thorax when they initially lacked that skill. citeturn314845search0turn314845search4

And experimentally increasing abdominal stiffness during walking changes thorax–pelvis phase relationships and reduces relative rotation between them. citeturn314845search3

This is one reason I would **not** recommend simply maximally bracing while doing your exercise. Too much stiffness can reduce useful segmental freedom. Your interesting variable is probably:

**stable enough to preserve a trunk reference frame, but not so rigid that the pelvis and thorax become mechanically welded together.**

That distinction is likely central.

## The biggest hidden lever may be sensory reweighting, not abdominal strength

This is where your “core is my proprioceptive brain” joke becomes unexpectedly close to something physiologically interesting.

A 2026 experiment specifically tested how **vestibular and trunk-proprioceptive inputs contribute to trunk stabilisation at different walking speeds**. Twelve healthy adults received paraspinal vibration or electrical vestibular stimulation while sitting, standing and walking between 0.8 and 5.5 km/h. The contribution of both sensory channels changed with task and speed. Responses increased as walking went from very slow to moderate speeds; at higher speeds, responses to proprioceptive vibration declined while vestibular responses plateaued. citeturn314845search1

That is an important result for your idea.

Your exercise changes simultaneously:

- pelvis position,
- trunk acceleration,
- foot loading,
- head movement,
- vestibular input,
- spinal proprioception,
- hip proprioception,
- abdominal pressure,
- breathing pattern,
- gait speed.

Your nervous system therefore cannot rely on one fixed sensory solution. It has to keep **reweighting information in real time**. citeturn314845search1

And researchers can actually see the nervous system adapt when locomotion becomes mechanically unfamiliar. During split-belt walking, the initial destabilising exposure produced the largest changes in gait symmetry, electrodermal arousal and activity across prefrontal, premotor, sensorimotor and parietal regions; those responses diminished with repeated exposure as adaptation occurred. citeturn505614search10

That gives us a compelling candidate mechanism for your feeling of being unusually “anchored”:

**you may be forcing the CNS to solve a high-information postural problem continuously, which increases the salience of trunk position and body state.**

That is stronger scientifically than saying “core activation improves embodiment”.

There is also evidence that explicit abdominal activation improves resistance to sudden perturbation. In healthy adults standing on a translating surface, bracing or hollowing reduced trunk angular displacement by roughly **25–36%** in several segments compared with natural standing. citeturn314845search7turn680606search9

And both abdominal bracing and controlled expiration reduced lumbar acceleration during unexpected loading. citeturn680606search6

So the nervous system can indeed make the torso a more stable reference structure while other parts of the body are being perturbed.

That is very close to what you're deliberately generating yourself.

## Why sudden peak-speed walking can feel like an “unlock”

This may be the second major leverage point.

Fast walking does something psychologically and neurologically different from slow walking.

In young adults, auditory reaction time was approximately:

**457 ± 91 ms while walking slowly**

**423 ± 80 ms at preferred speed**

**396 ± 73 ms while walking 30% faster**

The differences were significant. citeturn505614search9

A similar experiment in healthy older adults also found faster reaction times while walking rapidly than at self-selected or slow speeds. citeturn505614search8

This does **not** prove that fast walking universally increases neural processing speed. But it does establish that locomotor velocity can alter reaction-time performance in real time. citeturn505614search8turn505614search9

Ordinary walking itself can alter cognition. In healthy young men, walking during a Stroop task produced faster responses and greater prefrontal oxygenation peaks than standing. citeturn505614search7

Now add short explosive acceleration.

Very brief maximal exercise can produce strong sympathetic effects. Ten six-second sprints significantly raised circulating adrenaline and noradrenaline; another classic experiment using repeated six-second maximal treadmill runs found an approximately **18-fold rise in plasma adrenaline** at peak. citeturn505614search0turn505614search6

Even a single 30-second maximal sprint performed after nighttime awakening temporarily increased noradrenaline and reduced subjective sleepiness in a small pilot experiment. citeturn505614search5

That gives a plausible explanation for your observation that a few seconds of urgent near-maximum movement leaves something “on” afterwards:

**there can be a residual autonomic activation state after the mechanical event ends.**

There is also a genuinely separate phenomenon called post-activation potentiation/performance enhancement. Brief muscular activation can temporarily change subsequent neuromuscular performance, though effects on whole-body sprinting are inconsistent and highly individual. Some studies show enhancement minutes later; others find no advantage. citeturn939774search0turn939774search1turn939774search9

Brief non-fatiguing contractions can also transiently alter corticospinal excitability; one experiment found markedly increased supraspinal relative to spinal excitability immediately after submaximal contractions, although the functional consequence was uncertain. citeturn939774search4

So I would distinguish three things you may be combining:

| What you feel | Candidate mechanism | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate readiness before the step | anticipatory postural organisation | **strong** |
| Faster reactions while moving fast | locomotor-speed/arousal interaction | **moderate–strong** |
| “Activation” lingering afterwards | autonomic arousal + possible motor potentiation | **moderate** |
| Core-specific neural “unlock” lasting afterwards | unknown | **not established** |

The interesting experimental question is whether **trunk-regulated fast walking produces a larger after-effect than equally fast ordinary walking**.

Nobody in the studies I found appears to have tested that exact comparison.

That is precisely where your idea becomes scientifically testable.

## The diaphragm–abdominal–pelvic-floor–glottis system is probably where your voice observation comes from

This is the part where your report became much more than a gait question.

You said that after doing this correctly:

> your voice suddenly projects, baseline volume gets deeper/resonant, and the diaphragm feels “unlocked”.

There is a real anatomical reason these systems interact.

The diaphragm participates in respiration **and** postural control. During repetitive limb movement, diaphragm and transversus abdominis can develop tonic activity while simultaneously being modulated by both breathing and movement. Intra-abdominal pressure increases in proportion to the reactive forces produced by the moving limb. citeturn394040search9

Pelvic-floor contraction is likewise not isolated. Intramuscular EMG showed that pelvic-floor contraction was accompanied by recruitment of transversus abdominis and internal oblique, and maximal pelvic-floor contractions increased intra-abdominal pressure. citeturn394040search8

So functionally, you are dealing with a pressure-regulation cylinder whose components include:

**diaphragm ↔ abdominal wall ↔ pelvic floor ↔ rib cage ↔ larynx/glottis**

—not six independent fitness muscles. citeturn394040search8turn394040search9

And phonation sits directly on top of that system.

Speech at higher loudness involves greater abdominal muscle activity, especially when upright and when speaking at low lung volume. citeturn935105search7

When healthy adults were instructed to speak louder, they achieved it using different combinations of increased starting lung volume and greater expiratory-muscle tension. citeturn935105search8

Professional classical singers show more independent coordination between rib cage and abdomen than untrained singers, including a characteristic **pre-phonatory inward movement of the abdomen**. That strategy is thought to increase intra-abdominal pressure and improve control of subglottal pressure. citeturn935105search4turn935105search5

Professional opera singers producing greater projection showed increased acoustic power in the 2–4 kHz range, a larger rib cage configuration and evidence of greater abdominal support while actually reducing mean expiratory airflow—suggesting more efficient projection rather than simply “blowing harder”. citeturn935105search11

Actors and trained speakers also differ from untrained speakers mainly in respiratory-related variables such as inspiratory volume, airflow and subglottal pressure rather than solely in the laryngeal source. citeturn935105search13

That makes your subjective effect quite plausible:

**changing the state of your trunk/diaphragm/abdominal pressure system immediately before speaking could change how easily you generate and meter subglottal pressure.**

But there is an important counterpoint.

Simply changing posture does **not** reliably increase voice loudness. In experienced singers, one controlled pilot study found no meaningful differences in loudness, aerodynamic variables or phonatory muscle EMG among several body postures. citeturn935105search0

So the active ingredient is probably not merely “standing better”.

Your intervention is more aggressive physiologically: **dynamic locomotion + abdominal regulation + respiratory mobilisation + arousal + altered rib-cage mechanics.**

That combination is much more plausible as a voice-state changer.

### The glottis connection is real — but don't chase maximal closure

Laryngeal closure commonly accompanies intense muscular effort. In a small endoscopic experiment, glottal closure occurred near the beginning of maximal upper-limb efforts, and preventing laryngeal closure was associated with lower power output. citeturn680606search1

Classic lifting studies also found that glottal closure helps maintain elevated intra-abdominal pressure. citeturn680606search0

But deliberately maximising glottal closure is **not** the intervention I would extract from this. Breath-holding and Valsalva raise intra-abdominal pressure but also substantially change cardiovascular loading, and breath control does not reliably increase maximal trunk force. citeturn680606search2turn680606search8

Your highest-ROI target is likely **pressure regulation**, not pressure maximisation.

Think:

**responsive diaphragm + active abdominal wall + mobile rib cage + stable pelvis + open enough airway to breathe and speak easily.**

## The overlooked intervention space

After sweeping this literature, I think there really *is* a neglected region here, although I would phrase it more conservatively than “the fitness industry has missed it completely”.

Researchers study its pieces under different labels:

**anticipatory postural adjustments**

**dynamic trunk stability**

**thorax–pelvis dissociation**

**sensory reweighting**

**locomotor adaptation**

**respiratory–postural coupling**

**subglottal pressure regulation**

**post-activation potentiation**

**embodied affect**

What is largely missing is an intervention that deliberately combines them.

The exercise you are converging on looks something like:

> **continuously regulated trunk orientation while deliberately varying pelvis freedom, vertical COM displacement, locomotor speed, acceleration and respiratory state.**

That is not a normal plank.

It is not conventional “core activation”.

It is not ordinary gait training.

It is closer to **whole-body sensorimotor perturbation training with a deliberately preserved axial reference frame**.

And it suggests several unusually high-value experimental levers.

| Lever | What to manipulate | What it may train |
|---|---|---|
| **Trunk regulation** | relaxed → lightly organised → moderately braced | axial reference stability |
| **Pelvis freedom** | normal gait → exaggerated pelvic rotation/dissociation | segmental independence |
| **Vertical oscillation** | ordinary walking → tiny repeated squat pulses | vertical force regulation |
| **Speed transitions** | slow → fast → near-fast → slow | anticipatory motor switching |
| **Acceleration onset** | gradual vs sudden | gait-initiation APA efficiency |
| **Step timing** | activate trunk just before first step vs after | preparatory control |
| **Breathing** | unrestricted vs deliberate long expiration | diaphragm/abdominal coordination |
| **Speech immediately after** | same phrase before/after | respiratory–phonatory transfer |
| **Eyes/head position** | stable vs natural scanning | vestibular contribution |
| **Duration** | 5 s, 15 s, 30 s bursts | threshold for after-effects |

The key is **not increasing every variable simultaneously**. If you do that, you learn nothing.

A much better experimental sequence would be:

**ordinary walk → regulated-core walk → regulated-core + vertical pulses → regulated-core + sudden fast burst → return to normal walk.**

Then observe whether each transition changes:

reaction readiness, perceived stability, arm freedom, breathing ease, voice volume, voice effort, spatial awareness and how long the effect lasts.

That would let you discover whether the “unlock” requires speed, vertical movement, trunk attention or some interaction among them.

## The highest-ROI protocol I would actually test

Based on the literature, the strongest version is probably **low-force, high-coordination**, not maximal tension.

Start walking normally for a minute. Then lightly organise the abdomen—enough that you can feel the trunk as a coherent unit but still breathe comfortably. Evidence from perturbation experiments shows that even moderate abdominal activation can reduce unwanted trunk movement; maximal bracing is unnecessary. citeturn680606search6turn314845search7

Then introduce 10–20 seconds of tiny knee/hip flexion pulses while keeping the head and upper trunk relatively quiet. This deliberately increases pelvis/leg movement beneath a regulated upper frame, conceptually similar to trunk–pelvis dissociation training. citeturn314845search0turn314845search4

Then return to ordinary gait for several seconds and suddenly accelerate toward a comfortably fast walking pace—**not an all-out sprint**—for 5–10 seconds. Fast walking itself increases trunk activation and can shorten reaction time relative to slow walking. citeturn890919search9turn505614search9

Then slow down while retaining the same sense of axial organisation.

Immediately afterwards, say the same short sentence you used before starting, at the same intended effort.

Do **not** deliberately clamp the glottis or force a Valsalva. Bracing can reduce lung volume, and excessive pressure strategies are unnecessary for what you're investigating. citeturn680606search4turn680606search2

What I would look for is not “does my voice get louder?” alone.

I would look for a **cluster**:

**lower perceived phonatory effort + easier volume + clearer low-frequency bodily vibration + longer phrase length + easier inhalation + reduced need to push the throat.**

That pattern would be much more consistent with improved respiratory–phonatory organisation than simply yelling harder. citeturn935105search5turn935105search8turn935105search14

There is also a psychologically interesting reason this may feel unusually potent. Walking itself can increase positive affect, while upright walking under stress has been associated with lower sleepiness and improved subjective state compared with slumped walking. citeturn126438search0turn126438search3

Alternating standing and sitting has also been shown to acutely increase arousal and processing speed relative to prolonged sitting, without improving every executive function. citeturn126438search8

So the “command feels in the body” effect may arise from **several systems becoming congruent at once**:

motor readiness,

upright postural orientation,

sympathetic activation,

more active respiratory support,

more efficient subglottal-pressure regulation,

and increased interoceptive/proprioceptive salience.

That is a much richer hypothesis than “core engagement gives confidence”.

## What looks genuinely new here

The strongest scientific claim I can make after this audit is **not** that you've discovered a proven hidden biological switch.

It is this:

**The literature already demonstrates almost every component of your experience independently, but researchers usually study them in separate silos.**

Gait researchers study anticipatory trunk control. citeturn890919search1

Motor-control researchers study trunk–pelvis dissociation. citeturn314845search0

Sensory researchers study speed-dependent proprioceptive/vestibular weighting. citeturn314845search1

Exercise physiologists study arousal and post-activation effects. citeturn505614search0turn939774search4

Voice researchers study abdominal–rib-cage control and subglottal pressure. citeturn935105search5turn935105search11

Posture researchers study affect and arousal. citeturn126438search0turn126438search8

What is conspicuously under-investigated is **deliberately using locomotion to perturb the body while consciously preserving trunk regulation, then measuring immediate transfer into reaction speed, bodily readiness, respiratory control and voice production**.

That is the repository.

And the variable I would put at the centre of it is not “core strength”.

It is:

# **How rapidly can the nervous system preserve or recreate an axial reference frame while the rest of the body is being deliberately destabilised?**

That single variable could plausibly connect your original observations about rapid defensive readiness, pelvic mobility, explosive gait initiation, proprioceptive “anchoring”, the post-burst activation effect and the sudden change in vocal support.

The literature does **not yet prove that those are one biological phenomenon**.

But after this sweep, there is enough mechanistic overlap that treating them as a single research programme is scientifically defensible—and considerably more interesting than conventional core training.
